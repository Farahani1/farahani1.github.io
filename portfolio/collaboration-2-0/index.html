Background Story

Two-minute read. If you read nothing else, read this.

Background Context

It is a story of a digital-industries division of roughly 100 technical staff — industrial condition-monitoring products, IIoT, embedded and platform software. The division started in 2016 with about 20 experts and scientists. Given the high hopes — or maybe the apparent necessity — of keeping up with the trends of the so-called digital revolution, this R&D unit got support, expanded its projects and studies, and ended up with about 100 people working there by 2024. Its customers were mostly other parts of the same industrial group, plus some external plant operators.

Then in 2026, with the national economy under severe pressure, the authorities suddenly zoomed in on the division and questioned whether the programme had succeeded. And here is the interesting part. There was an ongoing debate, with a wide spread of opinion, on the answer to “was it a failure?” As the wise might expect, a definite answer to that question is not the important side of the argument. Nevertheless, if you take the financial return to date as the central criterion, it is hard to answer anything other than “Yes”.

In the middle of that sudden curiosity from the higher decision makers, and their strong will to prune the system (rather like Elon Musk swinging a chainsaw over his head), the engineers and developers were the main focus of the blame. It was no big surprise that they were the ones most likely to be pruned first. Then I brought up an argument — which I believe almost nobody could defeat — showing that the problem is not the employees. It is rooted somewhere else, around the very hand holding the chainsaw.

An Important Lesson

That was the glorious moment when Hossein decided to become the Nelson Mandela of the group :))) (see 4.1 · Evidence — the intellectual-assets analysis) NEVERTHELESS, it didn’t take long for him to realize that the system was asking for more of an Ayatollah Khomeini or a Reza Khan! (See Spoiler)

That is why I believe that if you miss the nuances of managing projects like this, the final bill is bigger than the wasted time and money — you end up paying in culture. And once a bad culture settles in, uprooting it is far harder than getting the management right in the first place.

What did I do?

Diagnosis

I have known the organization since I was a member of the APAC Research Group during my M.Sc. The research group worked with them from time to time, so I was always aware of their internal processes and projects. My observations always set me thinking about how the concepts and ideas I study in complex systems and socio-cultural systems could explain the repeated patterns and the systematic malfunctioning of this one.

The visible symptoms were the ordinary ones, which you have probably witnessed yourself from time to time: slipped schedules, duplicated work, friction at integration points, products that reached the market without anyone able to say who the customer was. The diagnosis was that these were not independent problems. They were being driven by an underlying mechanism.

Intervention

At some point I got the chance to put my ideas to the division’s leaders, and we found that we could build a dialogue and a shared understanding effectively. That encouraged me to take a couple of steps further, write a structured analysis, and propose a solution named Collaboration 2.0. It is deliberately not a culture program. It is a change to the authoring process of a single document — the Product Requirement Document — chosen on the reasoning that an organization can be made to adopt a document, but cannot be made to adopt trust. The PRD was the wedge; transparency, distributed decision-making, and traceable credit were smuggled in as properties of its workflow rather than declared as values.

This work grew into a phased 24-month strategy, a concrete process design, document templates, and a role and responsibility model mapped to ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288.

Spoiler

I should say straight away what all my efforts and struggles for change ended up producing. As a matter of fact, it remained a half-executed plan. It was disrupted — interestingly — by the same factor that had been identified as the root of the causes.

Now that I look back at this experience, it reminds me of Richard Dawkins’s selfish gene: a particular code of behaviour in the cell’s propagation suppresses other genes that carry a competing function. Systematically, that behaviour steers the whole platform the molecule sits in — a far higher level of biological organization — in the direction that reinforces the molecule itself and keeps it present in future generations.

The framework I proposed comes out of my system-oriented thinking, and especially my bias toward framing any problem with people in it as a complex system — a socio-cultural system. One common example of how a minor change at the micro level ends up as a huge change in the macro pattern is a fish swarm: adjust one behavioral variable in each fish slightly, such as the tendency to turn toward the center of the population, and the dynamics and the shape of the circling swarm change.

Collaboration 2.0 is designed on a similar mindset. It means to change the bigger patterns with changes that are:

  • small,
  • frequent,
  • widely spread,
  • and placed so that they block the causal relations in the graph discussed earlier.

Collaboration 2.0 is centered on transparency, deliberation, and the availability of contribution. The immediate outputs of those features are visibility, trackability, and therefore responsibility — and, alongside them, the harvesting of the company’s intellectual assets, which has always been the factor I weighted most heavily in the analysis.
